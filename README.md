# THE MONITORING TAX

## The Developmental Arc of Repeated Commission-Ejection Cycles, the Overfitted Suppression Strategy, the Preemptive Recognition Response, and the Stable Suboptimal State Both Parties Converge On When the Mechanism Runs Long Enough

*Eric Ren · ERI Labs · Jersey City, New Jersey · 2026 · github.com/ericrenone*

---

> "What begins as a deliberate, effortful response to a recurring social situation can, with sufficient repetition under consistent conditions, become automatic — triggered by the relevant environmental features without intention, awareness, or effort. The response is then difficult to override even when the original situation is absent." — Bargh, J.A., & Chartrand, T.L. (1999). The unbearable automaticity of being. *American Psychologist, 54*(7), 462–479.

> "The capacity to regulate oneself draws on a finite resource. When self-regulation is exercised in one domain — to manage a threat, to modulate an expression, to suppress an impulse — that resource is depleted. The depletion is not domain-specific. It impairs subsequent self-regulation across different demands in different contexts." — Muraven, M., & Baumeister, R.F. (2000). Self-regulation and depletion of limited resources. *Psychological Bulletin, 126*(2), 247–259.

> "People with a chronic prevention focus are especially sensitive to losses and failures, orient toward avoidance strategies, and narrow the range of means they consider available. The prevention orientation is not stable by nature — it is recruited by situations that establish safety as the governing concern. But once recruited repeatedly in a specific domain, it becomes the default motivational stance the person brings to that domain." — Higgins, E.T. (1997). Beyond pleasure and pain. *American Psychologist, 52*(12), 1280–1300.

> "Psychological distance from a target — social, temporal, spatial — does not merely attenuate an emotional response. It changes the cognitive structure of the representation. Near targets are represented concretely and contextually; the same target at distance is represented abstractly and in terms of its superordinate features. The shift from near to far is a shift from 'what this is' to 'what it means.'" — Trope, Y., & Liberman, N. (2010). Construal-level theory of psychological distance. *Psychological Review, 117*(2), 440–463.

## I. The Gap This Document Addresses

The prior documents in this lineage establish with considerable empirical precision what happens in a single commission-ejection cycle: how the Commissioned Non-Reception is structured, how the Comprehension Threshold Trigger fires, how the Self-Evaluation Maintenance Ejection executes through the Proxy Commission Gap, how the Domain Fortress Response seals the domain, and how the Terminal Concealment removes the contribution from the shared record. The six-framework arc is complete as a static account of a single episode.

It is not complete as a developmental account of what happens when the episode repeats.

And it repeats. The configuration that produces the commission-ejection cycle — acknowledged competence, proximate context, self-relevant domain, comparison threat — is not a rare conjunction. It is the structural condition of every close collaborative relationship in which the parties differ meaningfully in capacity. Families produce this configuration continuously across childhood and adolescence, because siblings share context, evaluative audience, and overlapping domains. Workplaces produce it whenever a superior performer is embedded in a team where their superiority is proximate and visible. Academic settings produce it in peer collaboration, in advising relationships, in every context where demonstrated capacity can be compared.

The outlier who has experienced one commission-ejection cycle will, in virtually any sustained proximate relationship with a capacity-differential, experience it again. The peer who deployed the ejection once has demonstrated — to themselves, without consciously registering the demonstration — that ejection is an effective tool for managing comparison threat. What both parties learned from the first cycle shapes how they approach every subsequent encounter.

This is the temporal dimension the existing account omits. The prior frameworks document a sequence. They do not document what the sequence deposits in both parties, how those deposits alter behavior across subsequent encounters, what the accumulated deposits cost in cognitive terms, and where the developmental trajectory of both parties terminates when the deposits have compounded across enough iterations.

This document addresses those questions. The central findings are:

The outlier develops, across repeated ejection episodes, a monitoring system calibrated to detect and pre-empt comparison triggers. This system imposes a compounding cognitive overhead — the **monitoring tax** — that diverts executive resources from task performance. The system becomes increasingly automatic, and increasingly miscalibrated to the proximate-threat context that trained it, eventually generalizing to suppress performance in non-proximate, non-competitive contexts where suppression is neither needed nor appropriate.

The peer develops, across repeated ejection episodes, an increasingly efficient recognition-and-ejection schema that eventually becomes preemptive: the peer learns to recognize the conditions that will require ejection before they materialize, and stops issuing the commissions that would trigger them. The commissioning behavior that was once motivated by the acknowledgment of the outlier's competence is now inhibited by that same acknowledgment.

Both developmental arcs converge on a state that neither party would have chosen: **mutual suppression**. The outlier no longer expresses their full capacity in the proximate context because the monitoring system prevents it. The peer no longer commissions the outlier's capacity because the preemptive recognition schema has identified the pattern as reliably costly. The comparison that the ejection mechanism was designed to forestall no longer occurs. Neither does the collaboration. Both parties are producing less than they would have produced had the mechanism not run long enough to reach its stable terminus.

This is the **stable suboptimal**: the developmental endpoint of a mechanism that works precisely as intended, arriving at an outcome neither party intended.

## II. The Cycle as Learning Event

What does the commission-ejection cycle actually teach both parties?

The question is not about conscious learning. Neither the peer who deploys the ejection nor the outlier who experiences it sits down afterward to draw explicit lessons. The learning in question is the kind that operates below the level of deliberate instruction: associative, implicit, conditional, and — precisely because of this — resistant to conscious override.

For the peer, the cycle teaches a conditional association: [proximate context + high-capacity performance in self-relevant domain] → [threat] → [ejection] → [resolution of threat]. Each time the ejection terminates the comparison threat, the association between ejection and threat resolution is reinforced. This is negative reinforcement in the technical sense: an aversive state — the SEM comparison threat — is terminated by a specific behavior — the ejection — and the behavior is strengthened. The peer does not choose to become a more efficient ejector. They become one, in the same way that any reliably reinforced behavior becomes more available, more quickly triggered, and more automatically executed with each reinforcement trial.

Over successive cycles, the recognition-triggering features are more finely discriminated. Early in the cycle history, the ejection fires late — after the Comprehension Threshold Trigger has fully activated, after the demonstration is well underway. Later in the cycle history, the recognition fires earlier — at the initial cues of the CTT-activating dynamic. Eventually, the peer can identify the incoming configuration before it has fully materialized. At this stage of schema refinement, the response is preemptive: the peer does not commission at all.

This is not deliberate discrimination. It is the efficiency that comes from sufficient practice. Logan (1988) documented the transition from algorithm-based to instance-based processing in cognitive tasks; Bargh and Chartrand (1999) extended it to social behavior. The peer's recognition-and-ejection schema follows the same developmental trajectory. What required effortful deliberation early in the cycle history has become, after sufficient repetitions, an automatic pre-threat response.

For the outlier, the cycle teaches a different association: [visible demonstration of capacity] → [ejection] → [loss of collaboration, contribution, credit]. And its corollary: [modulated demonstration] → [continued relationship]. The behavioral implication follows without explicit calculation: modulate demonstration, and the aversive outcome is avoided.

Coleman and Cross (2000) documented this as the façade of normalcy — the strategic dampening of performance to maintain proximate relationships. What the existing account does not develop is the learning curve of the concealment strategy itself. The first concealment is effortful, deliberate, and imperfect. Later concealments are smoother. Eventually, the monitoring system that manages the concealment operates at near-automatic levels. This is precisely when its full developmental costs begin to accumulate.

## III. The Outlier's Developmental Arc: From Reactive Concealment to Architectural Suppression

Early in the cycle history, the outlier's concealment is reactive. Demonstration occurs, ejection fires, the outlier notices the pattern, and next time they modulate. This is post-hoc learning applied forward — conscious, deliberate, requiring explicit attention to each episode's features.

As the concealment strategy matures, it moves upstream. Rather than modulating after noticing the threat cues, the outlier begins monitoring for threat cues before beginning the demonstration, calibrating the output's level to the perceived threat before the demonstration is fully expressed. The monitoring task now runs in parallel with the performance task.

This is the developmental transition from reactive concealment to **architectural suppression**: the outlier has built an ongoing monitoring system that runs as a background process in every proximate performance context. The monitoring system's job is to continuously assess: Is this audience threat-configured? Would full demonstration trigger the ejection? What is the appropriate level of modulation for this context?

Three findings in the cognitive performance literature converge on the cost of this system.

Schmader and Johns (2003), extending Steele and Aronson's (1995) stereotype threat paradigm, established that performance suppression under social threat operates through working memory depletion. When a social threat is active — when the person performing is simultaneously monitoring how their performance will be received in a threat-configured context — executive resources are divided between the performance task and the monitoring task. Both draw from the same finite pool. What goes to monitoring is not available for performance. Performance degrades not because ability is absent but because the cognitive resources that would have served it have been recruited for self-protection.

Muraven and Baumeister (2000) established the resource model of self-regulation: effortful self-control draws on a limited resource, and the exercise of that resource in one domain impairs subsequent performance in any domain requiring self-regulation. The monitoring system is a form of sustained self-regulation: the outlier is continuously regulating their expressive behavior. The resource cost is not domain-specific. It impairs self-regulatory capacity across subsequent demands — including demands that have nothing to do with the proximate comparison context.

Bargh and Chartrand (1999) and Logan (1988) established that repeatedly practiced responses become automatic and execute without requiring deliberative resources. When the monitoring system has been sufficiently practiced, it begins executing automatically: the outlier enters any performance context and the monitoring system fires, not because the context is specifically threatening but because the monitoring trigger has been associated with performance contexts generally. At this stage, the monitoring tax is not a cost incurred only in threatening contexts. It is a cost incurred in all performance contexts.

Together, these findings establish the monitoring tax as a developmental process with three stages:

**Stage 1 — Deliberate Monitoring:** The outlier consciously attends to threat cues and modulates deliberately. Cost is high per episode; low between episodes.

**Stage 2 — Semi-Automatic Monitoring:** The monitoring system runs as a background process in proximate-threat contexts. Cost is moderate and persistent within those contexts.

**Stage 3 — Architectural Suppression:** The monitoring system runs automatically across all performance contexts, regardless of whether the specific context is threat-configured. Cost is persistent across contexts, including those where it produces no benefit.

The developmental trajectory is from Stage 1 toward Stage 3 with repeated exposure to the commission-ejection cycle. Stage 3 is the stable state: the outlier carries a monitoring overhead calibrated to the proximate-threat context that now fires everywhere.

## IV. The Overfitting Problem: When the Right Lesson Becomes the Wrong Rule

Stage 3 generalization is the most consequential — and most overlooked — consequence of the repeated cycle.

To understand why generalization is damaging, it is necessary to be precise about what kind of context the monitoring system was calibrated to. The contexts that trained the system share a specific configuration: proximate audience, high psychological closeness, self-relevant domain, comparison threat active. The ejection dynamic the monitoring system learned to preempt is specifically a proximate-threat-configured dynamic. The SEM-E fires under high closeness and high self-relevance; it does not fire under low closeness or in domains not central to the observer's self-concept.

The monitoring system cannot distinguish these configurations at Stage 3, because the associative learning that drove automatization did not encode the contextual features that determine *when* ejection is threatening. It encoded "performance context" as the trigger, not "proximate-high-relevance performance context." This is overfitting in the machine learning sense: the model has been trained on a specific distribution of examples — proximate-threat contexts — and generalizes poorly to the out-of-distribution context — non-proximate, non-threat-configured evaluative contexts.

The outlier's monitoring system has learned the right lesson for the proximate context and is applying it everywhere.

## V. The Construal Distance Paradox: Why the Monitoring System Fires Where It Doesn't Need To

Trope and Liberman's (2010) Construal Level Theory provides the theoretical architecture for understanding what distinguishes the contexts the monitoring system was calibrated to from the contexts in which it now misfires.

CLT establishes that psychological distance along any dimension — social, temporal, spatial, hypothetical — changes how objects are mentally represented. Near objects are represented concretely: with their specific, contextual, low-level features foregrounded. Distant objects are represented abstractly: with their superordinate, decontextualized, high-level features foregrounded.

The proximate audience — the sibling, the colleague, the near-peer — represents the outlier's capacity concretely: *this* person, *right here*, doing *this* thing, in *this* domain where I need to be measured favorably. The concrete representation activates the SEM-E because the threat is immediate, specific, and high-relevance. It is a verdict about the self, not a fact about another person.

The distant audience — the conference reviewer, the future reader, the specialist in an adjacent field who encounters the outlier's published work — represents the outlier's capacity abstractly: *someone who does this kind of work well*, interesting, potentially relevant. The abstract representation does not activate the SEM-E because the components that fire it — high closeness, immediate comparison, contingent self-relevance — are absent. It is information about the world, not measurement of the self.

The monitoring system, trained exclusively on proximate-threat contexts, has learned to fire on the concreteness signal that those contexts reliably produced. In Stage 3 architectural suppression, the automatic monitoring does not distinguish between proximate and distant audiences. It fires on "performance context" as the trigger. Because many evaluative contexts carry some degree of concreteness — the reviewer is a real person, the audience is present, the stakes are real — the monitoring fires.

The result is the **Construal Distance Paradox**: the outlier's monitoring system suppresses most reliably in the contexts where full demonstration would be most welcomed, most useful, and most likely to generate genuine credit — the distant, non-competitive, evaluatively positioned contexts that the SEM-E has no functional reason to activate in.

The conference reviewer who might have recognized a cross-paradigm synthesis receives a modulated version. The publication that could have constituted the public record of the outlier's analytical architecture is calibrated downward to a level the monitoring system determined was safe — a level calibrated to the proximate audience that trained the system, not to the distant audience actually evaluating the work. The external assessment, the professional recognition, the archival record — all the contexts where the outlier's contribution could at last escape the proximate social dynamics and be evaluated on its merits — are precisely the contexts where the overfitted monitoring system intervenes.

The concealment strategy learned in the proximate context defeats the outlier in every context where escape from the proximate context would have been possible.

## VI. The Regulatory Reorientation: Prevention Focus and Its Long-Term Suppressive Effects

Higgins's (1997) Regulatory Focus Theory distinguishes two motivational orientations: the promotion focus, orienting the person toward advancement, growth, and the presence of positive outcomes; and the prevention focus, orienting toward safety, security, and the absence of negative outcomes. Both are normal; they produce different strategic preferences, different emotional signatures, and different long-term developmental profiles.

What repeated commission-ejection cycles produce in the outlier, across sufficient iterations, is a chronic shift toward **prevention focus in the performance domain** — a shift from "how far can I go" to "how do I avoid triggering the response again." This shift is not chosen; it is the motivational consequence of a domain in which the governing concern has been, for long enough, the avoidance of a specific aversive outcome.

Shah, Higgins, and Friedman (1998) established that prevention-focused motivation produces adequate performance on tasks where meeting a standard is the goal, but specifically impairs performance on tasks requiring creative extension beyond established standards. Novelty, synthesis, frame-breaking, cross-domain abduction: these are promotion-focus performances. They require operating above the safe, proven, standard-meeting level. Prevention focus contracts the range of considered moves to those most certain not to produce loss.

For the outlier operating under Stage 3 architectural suppression with a chronically prevention-focused orientation in performance contexts, the impairment is not merely in quantity of expressed capacity but in kind. The kind of performance most suppressed is precisely the cross-paradigm synthesis, the novel reframing, the creative extension that exceeds established patterns — which, as THE-REINSTATEMENT-PREMIUM established, carries the highest economic and intellectual premium in the current transition. The monitoring tax is not only a working-memory cost. It is a motivational reorientation that suppresses the specific kind of performance that would be most valuable.

Dweck's (1986) distinction between performance goals and learning goals adds the developmental dimension. Performance goals orient the person toward demonstrating competence; learning goals orient toward developing it. Threat shifts orientation from learning to performance. The outlier who has internalized the commission-ejection dynamic's lesson — demonstrating triggers ejection — has been trained into a performance orientation: the goal is not to develop or to express fully, but to demonstrate at precisely the level that avoids the aversive outcome. This orientation is antithetical to the exploratory, failure-tolerant engagement through which cross-paradigm capacity develops.

The monitoring tax's most consequential cost may be the one least visible: the forestalled development of the very capacities that would have emerged from the exploratory engagement the prevention-focused orientation prevents.

## VII. The Peer's Developmental Arc: From Reactive Ejection to Preemptive Recognition

The peer's developmental trajectory is less examined and, in some respects, more consequential for the collaborative relationship's eventual structure.

The ejection, in the SEM model, is described as the third response strategy — available when the first two are foreclosed by circumstance. In a single episode, this is accurate: the ejection is reactive, the last option remaining. Across repeated episodes with the same outlier, the ejection is increasingly less reactive. The peer has accumulated enough pattern information to predict the ejection-configured situation at its early stages — not at the moment of CTT activation, but at the moment the commission is being considered.

The peer now possesses a model of the outlier that generates a specific prediction: if I commission this, the demonstration will exceed the comprehension threshold, the SEM-E will fire, and the ejection will be required. The implicit cost-benefit calculation, running below the level of deliberate reasoning, produces the same output each time: don't commission.

This is the **preemptive commissioning withdrawal** — the peer stops initiating collaborations with the outlier in the domains where the acknowledgment of the outlier's competence was once sufficient to motivate the ask. The acknowledgment does not diminish. What changes is the association between the acknowledgment and the decision to commission. Over repeated episodes, the acknowledgment — "this person is better at this than I am" — has been consistently paired with the ejection outcome. The commissioning behavior, once motivated by the acknowledgment, is now inhibited by it.

This is a reversal of the original dynamic's logic. The commission was motivated by the recognition of the outlier's superiority. The preemptive recognition response means that the same recognition now demotivates the commission. The outlier who is most qualified to help is now the least likely to be asked, because being asked has reliably produced an outcome the peer's learning history associates with aversive consequence.

Fiske and Neuberg's (1990) continuum model of impression formation documents how social perceivers move from category-based to individuated processing as they accumulate information about a specific target. The peer's movement toward preemptive recognition is a form of individuated processing: rather than applying the generic "high-competence person" script (which includes commissioning), the peer has built a specific model of this outlier that predicts the ejection-requiring demonstration. Individuated processing is typically considered more accurate social cognition. Here, it is accurate — and its accuracy is precisely what makes the commissioning stop.

The preemptive schema also generalizes. As with the outlier's monitoring system, the peer's recognition schema becomes associated with a broader class of triggers than the original context specified. Over sufficient repetitions, the peer begins recognizing "outlier-type" patterns not only in this specific person but in configurations that resemble the training context. High-confidence, cross-domain, structurally different performance in a self-relevant area: the schema recognizes it before the specific person has been assessed, and the avoidance behavior is initiated in advance.

The peer's Stage 3 equivalent is **preemptive pattern avoidance**: the peer stops entering collaborative configurations that pattern-match to the commissioning-ejection dynamic, regardless of whether the specific other party is the outlier who trained the schema.

## VIII. The Fortress Plateau: What the Sealed Domain Costs the Peer Over Time

The Domain Fortress Response seals the domain against comparative exposure. This is correctly identified, in the existing account, as protective. What is not developed is what the sealed domain costs the peer across time.

A domain sealed against comparative exposure is a domain sealed against calibration.

Calibration — the process by which a performer gains accurate information about where their performance stands relative to the frontier — requires comparison with performers at or near the frontier. This is not merely motivational; it is informational. Without calibration, the performer's internal standard drifts. They may improve relative to their own prior performance while failing to track whether that improvement tracks the field. They may plateau below the frontier without knowing they have plateaued.

The peer who seals the domain against the outlier has sealed it against the most calibrating input available: performance that demonstrably exceeds their own in the same domain. The ejection that protected the self-evaluation in the short term has, in the medium term, impaired the self-evaluation's accuracy. The fortress is comfortable and increasingly incorrect.

Dunning, Johnson, Ehrlinger, and Kruger (2003), in their synthesis of metacognitive accuracy research, establish that people with the weakest skills in a domain consistently overestimate their own performance — driven by the same metacognitive deficit that makes the skill weak. Without external calibrating comparison, internal competence estimates drift toward overestimation. The fortress removes the external calibration mechanism. The peer's self-assessment, unmoored from accurate comparison, inflates.

Crocker and Park (2004) document that domain fortification, while short-term protective, consistently forecloses the learning opportunities that the threatening comparison would have enabled. What the **fortress plateau** adds is the directionality of the foreclosure: the peer's performance trajectory in the sealed domain bends downward — not immediately, but progressively — as the absence of calibrating input reduces the accuracy of the self-assessment that governs performance decisions. The same ejection that terminated the comparison threat has, on a longer timescale, replaced the threat of accurate negative comparison with the slower accumulation of inaccurate positive comparison. A false security whose debt comes due whenever the sealed domain is exposed to external evaluation.

The peer who has ejected the outlier and fortified the domain is not protected from the comparison. They are protected from the *knowledge* of the comparison's extent. The gap is still there. It is simply no longer measured.

## IX. The Convergence: Where Both Developmental Arcs Terminate

The outlier's arc: monitoring tax compounds across the cycle history, system generalizes from proximate-threat contexts to all performance contexts, Stage 3 architectural suppression establishes, prevention-focused motivational orientation becomes chronic, full capacity is not expressed in the proximate context and is modulated even in distant contexts where modulation is counterproductive.

The peer's arc: recognition-and-ejection schema becomes preemptive, commissioning withdrawal eliminates the mechanism by which the comparison would be initiated, the domain fortress produces calibration drift and fortress plateau, schema generalizes to outlier-type configurations beyond the specific individual.

Both arcs converge on the stable suboptimal.

The stable suboptimal is distinguished from the immediate outcome of a single ejection episode by its equilibrium properties. After a single episode, the situation could in principle be disrupted: the commission could be reissued, the outlier could calibrate the demonstration differently, the peer could, under different circumstances, receive the help otherwise. The single-episode outcome is not stable — changing circumstances can displace it.

The stable suboptimal is not displaced by changing circumstances, because the learning deposits in both parties generate the same output regardless of circumstance. The peer's preemptive recognition schema fires before the commission is considered. The outlier's monitoring system fires before the demonstration is attempted. The commission-ejection event never materializes, because both parties' learned responses have moved upstream of the point at which the event would occur.

In this equilibrium, the comparison threat that drove the mechanism is gone. Neither party is being compared to the other in the domain because neither party is initiating the context in which comparison would occur. The mechanism has achieved its function perfectly. The comparison is permanently forestalled. And so is the collaboration that was supposed to serve both parties' aims.

Both parties are operating, in what was once a shared domain, below their capacity: the outlier through monitored suppression; the peer through calibration drift in the fortress plateau. The shared output that the commission was supposed to enable is absent. Both parties would have produced more — separately, together, or in any other configuration — if the mechanism had not run long enough to reach the stable suboptimal.

This is the structure of a tragedy that is not a failure of anyone's character or intention. The peer's preemptive recognition is rational given their learning history. The outlier's architectural suppression is adaptive given theirs. The stable suboptimal is the Nash equilibrium of two locally rational developmental trajectories whose aggregate outcome neither party would have selected.

## X. The Stable Suboptimal's Invisibility: Why Intervention Becomes Impossible at the Terminal State

There is a property of the stable suboptimal that the single-episode account does not predict and that has significant practical implications: at the stable suboptimal, the mechanism that produced it is invisible.

The commission-ejection mechanism, in a single episode, produces observable events: the commission, the demonstration, the ejection, the concealment. These events are legible — to the outlier, to third parties, potentially to the peer in a reflective moment. An intervention targeting the mechanism at this stage has something to target: the commission could be structured differently, the demonstration could be calibrated, the ejection could be named and examined.

At the stable suboptimal, none of these events are occurring. There is no commission to examine. There is no demonstration to calibrate. There is no ejection to name. The observable signature of the mechanism — the commission-ejection arc — has disappeared, because both parties' developmental trajectories have moved the action upstream of all observable events. What remains is a relationship in which neither party commissions or demonstrates in the domain, which is indistinguishable, from any external vantage, from a relationship in which no commission was ever appropriate.

The mechanism's invisibility at the stable suboptimal is not accidental. It is the developmental output of two processes — preemptive recognition and architectural suppression — that are specifically designed (by the learning history, not by deliberate intent) to prevent the events that would make the mechanism observable. The stable suboptimal is, in the most precise sense, self-concealing.

This has a sharp implication for intervention. The commission-ejection mechanism can be interrupted. But the window for interruption is the period before the stable suboptimal is reached — before the peer's schema has become preemptive and before the outlier's monitoring has become architectural. Once both parties have fully entered Stage 3, the observable events that an intervention would target have ceased to occur. There is nothing left to intervene in.

Interventions directed at the stable suboptimal — attempts to restart the commission, to re-establish the collaborative relationship, to re-open the sealed domain — encounter the full force of both parties' Stage 3 automatizations. The peer's recognition schema identifies the restart attempt as the pattern associated with the ejection-requiring dynamic. The outlier's monitoring system fires preemptively in the reconstituted context. The stable suboptimal is structurally resistant to disruption from within, because the disruption attempt pattern-matches to the training context that produced the stability.

The only effective intervention point is before the stable suboptimal is reached. This requires identifying the commission-ejection cycle while it is still producing observable events — which requires that someone outside the dyad be attending to those events with the framework to name them. In the typical institutional and relational context, that attention is not present. The mechanism runs to completion.

## XI. The Developmental Dimension of the Reinstatement Problem

THE-REINSTATEMENT-PREMIUM established that the global economy is systematically misallocating human capital toward machine-dominated cognitive domains, and that the humans capable of the operations AI cannot perform — cross-paradigm synthesis, abductive problem framing, formal output verification — represent an undersupplied reinstatement channel whose development would carry large and compounding economic returns.

THE-TRAINED-HORIZON established that the institutional evaluation apparatus — grant scoring systems, peer review matching, literature discovery tools — is being trained on filtered data that systematically underrepresents translation-architect work, encoding the paradigm's blind spot as a computational preference and progressively reducing the probability that cross-paradigm synthesis survives evaluation.

The monitoring tax analysis adds a third layer: the suppression is not only institutional and computational. It is **developmental**, and it operates upstream of both the institutional filter and the computational apparatus, in the proximate relational contexts — families, schools, early career environments — where the cross-paradigm capacity would have been built, expressed, and credited before the person who might exercise it reaches either filtering mechanism.

The outlier whose developmental history includes repeated commission-ejection cycles in proximate contexts arrives at the institutional evaluation apparatus carrying Stage 3 monitoring overhead, chronically prevention-focused orientation in performance domains, and an overfitted suppression strategy that misfires in exactly the non-proximate, distant-audience contexts where institutional evaluation occurs. The compound filter that THE-TRAINED-HORIZON identifies operates on a population whose capacity expression has already been partly suppressed by the pre-institutional developmental mechanism this document describes.

The three analyses together establish a complete account of how cross-paradigm capacity is suppressed across the full developmental arc: first in the proximate relational contexts that shape both the capacity's development and the monitoring system that constrains its expression; then in the institutional evaluation apparatus that has been miscalibrated against its bibliographic signature; then in the computational apparatus that has been trained to remove it from the distribution its successors will learn from.

The reinstatement premium identified in THE-REINSTATEMENT-PREMIUM is not merely economically unrealized. It is developmentally suppressed before the economic realization becomes possible — suppressed by mechanisms that are locally rational for every party involved, globally suboptimal for the field, and invisible from within the stable suboptimal in which they terminate.

## XII. Novel Empirical Predictions

**P1 — The Monitoring Tax Working Memory Signature**
Outliers with documented histories of commission-ejection cycles (operationalized by peer-reported concealment, collaborations withdrawn, contribution non-acknowledgment across multiple episodes) will show lower available working memory capacity in proximate performance contexts than in matched non-proximate performance contexts, even when the tasks are identical. The gap should increase with the number of prior commission-ejection episodes. *Falsification condition:* No significant WM differential between proximate and non-proximate performance contexts in outlier-classified participants.

**P2 — The Overfitting Signature: Blind-Review Elevation**
Outliers from domains with high proximate-threat history will show performance modulation in distant, non-competitive evaluative contexts — blind review, anonymous assessment — that is inconsistent with their self-reported capacity and their proximate-context performance. Specifically, blind-context performance should be significantly higher than proximate-context performance on matched tasks, controlling for difficulty. The gap is the overfitting signature — the monitoring system firing unnecessarily in the non-proximate context. *Falsification condition:* No significant gap between blind-review performance and proximate-context performance in outlier-classified participants.

**P3 — The Preemptive Commissioning Withdrawal Trajectory**
Peers with documented ejection histories involving a specific outlier will show monotonically decreasing commissioning rates toward that outlier over successive collaborative opportunities — including in domains adjacent to, but not identical to, the original ejection domain. Schema generalization to adjacent domains is the specific prediction: the preemptive schema overreaches, as the outlier's monitoring system overreaches. *Falsification condition:* No decrease in commissioning rates for post-ejection versus pre-ejection periods in peer-outlier dyads with multiple collaborative opportunities across multiple domains.

**P4 — The Fortress Plateau Performance Trajectory**
Peers who show domain-fortress responses — concealment of post-ejection work, reduction in seeking comparative feedback, reduction in collegial exposure in the sealed domain — will show lower domain-performance growth rates over subsequent years than comparable peers who did not employ the fortress response following a comparison threat. Performance measured by output quality assessed by blind external evaluators, not self-report. *Falsification condition:* No significant performance growth differential between fortress-response and non-fortress-response peers at three-year follow-up.

**P5 — The Prevention Focus Shift and Creative Suppression**
Outliers with high monitoring-tax profiles will show significantly more prevention-focused motivation in their primary performance domains than outliers with low monitoring-tax profiles, measured by regulatory focus scales. Prevention focus will mediate the relationship between commission-ejection cycle history and creative performance output — specifically output rated high on novelty, cross-domain synthesis, and frame-departure from established paradigmatic standards. *Falsification condition:* No significant prevention-focus elevation in high-monitoring-tax outliers; or prevention focus does not mediate the relationship between cycle history and creative output quality.

**P6 — The Construal Distance Performance Gradient**
The monitoring system's overfitting to proximate-threat contexts should produce a specific performance gradient: outlier performance should be most suppressed in contexts that physically, relationally, or structurally resemble the proximate-threat training context, and least suppressed in contexts maximally dissimilar (anonymous, geographically or institutionally distant, novel audience type). Performance should increase monotonically with construal distance from the training context. *Falsification condition:* No significant relationship between context dissimilarity from prior ejection contexts and performance level on matched tasks.

**P7 — The Stable Suboptimal's Invisibility to Third Parties**
In assessments of peer-outlier relationship quality and collaborative output, third-party observers will show significantly lower ability to identify the commission-ejection history in dyads that have reached the stable suboptimal than in dyads still in active commission-ejection cycling. The stable suboptimal's behavioral signature — mutual non-commissioning, absence of demonstrated capacity in shared domains — is indistinguishable from the absence of a history, because the mechanism's observable events have ceased to occur. *Falsification condition:* Third-party observers correctly identify the stable-suboptimal dyads at rates significantly above chance.

**P8 — The Developmental Timing Effect on Monitoring Tax Magnitude**
Early-career commission-ejection cycles — before age 30 — will produce larger monitoring taxes than later-career cycles, because earlier cycles trained the monitoring system during periods of higher neurological plasticity and because the system accumulated more practice trials before Stage 3 automatization. Outliers classified by early versus late onset of the commission-ejection cycle history will differ significantly in monitoring tax measures and in the performance-suppression signature in non-proximate contexts. *Falsification condition:* No significant effect of cycle-onset timing on monitoring tax or performance-suppression measures.
